## 1. The cofactor identity holds in every transverse dimension

Let m≥1, A a real symmetric m×m matrix and B a symmetric direction. Put Δ=det A, J=adj A, Δ_B=Ddet(A)[B], and J_B=Dadj(A)[B]. Each is a polynomial in the entries of A and B.

For invertible A, differentiate J=ΔA⁻¹ in direction B:

    J_B = Δ_B A⁻¹ − Δ A⁻¹ B A⁻¹.

Multiplying by Δ and replacing ΔA⁻¹ by J gives

    Δ J_B = Δ_B J − J B J.                                      (A)

There is no division in (A). To establish it at any singular symmetric A, replace A by A+tI. This is invertible for all but finitely many real t. Every entry of the difference between the two sides is a polynomial in t, vanishes for infinitely many t, and therefore vanishes identically, including at t=0. This proves the singular extension for every m. In fact (A) also holds without symmetry; symmetry is used below when scalar products involving A⁻¹ are transposed.

For m=1, J=1, J_B=0 and Δ_B=B, so the identity becomes 0=B−B. Thus its lowest allowed dimension does not need a separate convention beyond adj([a])=[1]. The case m=0 (d=1) is outside this review's claim.

## 2. P₁, Q₁, V_o and the first-order cancellation

For invertible symmetric A, replacing J with ΔA⁻¹ in the source definition yields, term by term,

    P₁ = Δ² f₅/120 − Δ γᵀJη/12 + γᵀJBJγ/8
       = Δ²[f₅/120 − ηᵀA⁻¹γ/12 + γᵀA⁻¹BA⁻¹γ/8]
       = Δ²Q₁.                                                  (B)

The equality γᵀA⁻¹η=ηᵀA⁻¹γ uses symmetry. P₁ is polynomial on all symmetric A. Formula (B) identifies that polynomial with the rational expression on the invertible locus; it does not define a finite Q₁ at a singular matrix.

Write

    V_o = f₄ Δ_B/24 + f₅ Δ/120 − γᵀJη/12 − γᵀJ_Bγ/8,
    Y′ = f₄ Δ/12 − γᵀJγ/4.

Direct expansion gives

    12ΔV_o − 6Y′Δ_B
      = Δ²f₅/10 − ΔγᵀJη
        + (3/2)γᵀ(Δ_BJ − ΔJ_B)γ
      = Δ²f₅/10 − ΔγᵀJη + (3/2)γᵀJBJγ
      = 12P₁.                                                   (C)

In the first line the two f₄ΔΔ_B/2 terms cancel. The second line is (A). Consequently (C) is a polynomial identity and remains true at singular A.

On the invertible locus, let q=γᵀA⁻¹γ, z=f₄−3q and α₄=z/24. Substituting the differentiated inverse expression for J_B into V_o gives

    V_o = ΔQ₁ + (f₄/24 − q/8)Δ_B = ΔQ₁ + α₄Δ_B,
    Y′ = Δ(f₄−3q)/12 = 2Δα₄.                                   (D)

These are exactly the relations following Lemma Ω. They also explain (C): the α₄ terms cancel. The source correctly restricts (B) and (D) to invertible A.

## 3. Endpoint expansion and the absence of a cubic product coefficient

Use x along u and y in the m transverse coordinates. Set σ=−1 at M and σ=+1 at S, and

    α_σ=f_xx(σr/2)/r,
    β_σ=∇_y f_x(σr/2)/r,

    D_σ := det K_σ = α_σ det A_σ − r β_σᵀ adj(A_σ) β_σ.           (E)

(E) holds for singular A_σ: expand the bordered determinant by cofactors, or first prove it for invertible A_σ and apply the same polynomial-extension argument. No inverse or eigenvalue lower bound enters the determinant expansion.

The critical-point pins and height gap imply

    f₂ = −r² f₄/24 − r⁴ f₆/1920 + O(r⁶𝒩),
    f₃ = 12k − r² f₅/40 + O(r⁴𝒩),

Here 𝒩 controls the C⁹ norm; the source assumes more regularity than these particular terms need. Taylor expansion at σr/2 then gives

    α_σ = σ6k + r f₄/12 + σr² f₅/120 + r³ f₆/480 + O(r⁴𝒩),
    β_σ = σγ/2 + rη/12 + σr²θ/48 + O(r³𝒩),

where θ=∇_y∂_x⁴f(0) and E=∂_x³A(0). In particular the source's factors 1/120 and 1/12 require the pin corrections; using the raw endpoint Taylor coefficients 1/48 and 1/8 would be incorrect.

For the terms through r²,

    det A_σ = Δ + σrΔ_B/2 + r²(Δ_C+Δ_BB)/8 + O(r³𝒩ᴺ),

Substitution into (E) yields

which verifies both signs and both numerical factors in V.

It is important not to multiply two unstructured O(r³) remainders and simply declare an O(r⁴) product remainder. The missing information is the shared cubic coefficient. The expanded blocks above imply, for a polynomial Z in the relevant jets,

    D_σ = σa + rU + σr²V + r³Z + O(r⁴(1+k)𝒩ᴺ),  a=6kΔ.        (F)

    −D_−D_+ = a² + r²(2aV−U²) + O(r⁴(1+k)ᴺ𝒩ᴺ),

## 4. Formula (4.2), cusp substitution, and jet parity

## 2. Edge and interior terms both matter

Write t=a+s, H(t)=p(t)+p(-t), H'(t)=p'(t)-p'(-t), and M(t)=integral_{-t}^t p. Ordinary Leibniz differentiation, conditional on J'', gives

    I'(s) = [F(t)+B0*s] H(t) + B0*M(t),
    I''(s) = [F'(t)+2*B0] H(t) + [F(t)+B0*s] H'(t).

F(a)=32*kappa^2*Delta^2, F'(t)=-Delta^2*t/72. Therefore

    36*I'(0) = 1152*kappa^2*Delta^2[p(a)+p(-a)]
                +12*kappa*Delta^2 M(a) = Psi_kappa.

Thus delta*I'(0)=r*Q1*Psi_kappa. The two boundary contributions have the same sign, even for an asymmetric conditional density. The interior-weight term is also essential. The exact coefficients 1152 and 12 follow from these two different sources.

For |s|<=36*c_e*kappa, the explicit I'' formula gives the manuscript's bound

    |I''(s)| <= C*Delta^2*(kappa+kappa^2)
                 sup_{23*kappa<=|x|<=25*kappa} [p(x)+|p'(x)|].

This is a derivative of the one-dimensional Gaussian integral, not a derivative of an indicator-valued jet functional. Taylor's theorem hence controls the inside-cutoff conditional error by one half delta^2 times this bound.

## 3. Removing the cutoff also costs quadratically
