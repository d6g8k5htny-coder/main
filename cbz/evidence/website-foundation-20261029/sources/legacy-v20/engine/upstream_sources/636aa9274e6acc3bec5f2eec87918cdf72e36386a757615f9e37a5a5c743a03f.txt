                                                   Abstract. Let X(·) be a random field from RD to Rd , D ≥ d. We first studied
                                                   the level set X −1 (u), u ∈ Rd . In particular we gave a weak condition for this
                                                   level set to be rectifiable. Then, we established a Kac-Rice formula to compute
                                                   the (D − d) Hausdorff measure. Our results extend known results, particularly
                                                   in the non-Gaussian case where we obtained a very general result. We conclude
                                                   with several extensions and examples of application, including functions of
                                                   Gaussian random field, zeroes of the likelihood, gravitational microlensing,
                                                   shot-noise.


                                                                    1. Introduction and motivation
                                          1.1. History and the present of Kac-Rice formula roadmap. The study of
                                          level sets has begun with the pioneering works of Kac [22] and Rice [30] in the case
                                          of a random process R → R. Ito [21] gave the first general rigorous proof of what
                                          has become to be the Kac-Rice formula . A first general monograph was published
                                          by Cramer and Leadbetter [15]. At that time, only stochastic processes from
                                          R to R were considered. The applications were mainly in engineering [15] or
                                          oceanography [24].
                                              In 1957, Longuet-Higgings [24], for modeling the sea surface, established a Kac-
                                          Rice formula for the expectation of the length of the level set of a random field
                                          X : R2 → R. Its proof used an intuitive approach but very interesting ideas.
                                          Another interesting work is a paper by Brillinger [12] where he studied the zeros of
                                          a system of random equations and for the first time the celebrated area formula was
                                          used. Using this latter formula Brillinger established a Kac-Rice formula for almost
                                          every level (see Proposition 22 below) remarking that as long as both members are
                                          continuous, the equality for all levels is satisfied. However, his conditions for having
                                          this continuity are very strong; see his Corollary 3.1.
                                              The first convincing application to the expectation of the number of zeroes of
                                          random fields Rd → Rd was due to Adler [1] (in fact, Adler considered critical
                                          points that are the zeroes of the derivatives), and two different general proofs
                                          were published in [2] and [6].
                                              Another generalization is the case where dimensions disagree: that is, random
                                          fields RD → Rd , D > d. In such a case the level set is generically a set of
                                          dimension D − d. Defining and computing of the expectation of its measure
                                          are difficult problems. The first attempts seem to date back to Wschebor [32]
                                          and Cabaña [13]. The subject has been developed by Kratz-León [23], [2, Th

   In the last decades, new applications of the Kac-Rice formula have been
developed. The statistics high-dimensional and learning have presented the problem
of studying the landscape of random fields: the behavior of the number of critical
points with a given index on a high-dimensional sphere. This problem is related to
the Ising model [4].
   The random wave model due to Berry and the famous Berry cancellation prob-
lem [8] have been considerably studied in physics and astronomy. Many references
can be found in the recent review paper of Wigman [33] about the progress in the
study of nodal sets of random fields.
   In the 20th century, the Gaussian model was the most relevant: it permitted
to avoid technicalities but, above all, it was almost the only one under which pre-
cise calculations were doable. This explains why most of the manuals considered
mainly this case and some direct generalizations as χ2 processes. Nowadays, some
non-Gaussian models have been developed, for example, non-Gaussian likelihood
models, but the most common model is perhaps the shot-noise model. For applica-
tions of the KRF in these processes, see, for instance, the work of Biermé-Desolneux
[10].
   In the cited literature, there are numerous conditions that provide different proofs
of the Kac-Rice formulas. In this paper our goal is to provide simple proofs and
at the same time under conditions that are easy to verify and weaker than usual.
This is illustrated by the numerous examples given in Section 8.

   Let X : T → Rd be a random field defined on a open set T ⊂ RD .
   Given a fixed value u ∈ Rd , and a Borel subset B ⊂ T , we are interested in the

class C 1 a.s., and we denote by X ′ (·) its derivative.
   Irregular points of the level set Lu (B) are defined as points t in Lu (B), where the
derivative X ′ (t) is not a surjective linear map, i.e., when the generalized Jacobian

of the level set Lu for C 1 random fields. Note that this condition is needed in the

    Let us first consider the case D = d in that case the Bulinskaya condition reads:
a.s. there are no irregular points. In classical results, some assumptions are needed
to obtain this condition, for example, [2, Lemma (11.2.1)] or [7, Proposition 6,5].
The present proof, based on local time permits to get this condition for free and
even in the very classical Gaussian random fields, our condition for the Kac-Rice
formula are weaker. In addition, in our opinion, the proof is simpler.
    Let us turn to the case D > d. Under further smoothness conditions on the path
of X, the Morse-Sard type arguments and implicit function theorem imply that Lu
is a manifold for almost every level u ∈ Rd . However, our main interest is for a
prescribed value u, say, u = 0.
    It is sometimes written that, if the paths of X are C 1 the level set is always a
manifold, which to be true needs some additional regularity. This can be the case
for almost every level using the Morse-Sard theorem, as long as the random field
is sufficiently differentiable. Perhaps it was that situation that led to this assertion
in the work of Ibragimov–Zaporozhets [20]. However, this statement is not true in
full generality as shown by the following counter-example:
  Let D = 2 d = 1 and, for example,

(2.3)                    P {∃t ∈ U, X(t) = u, ∆(t) = 0} = 0,

   Our contribution is to introduce instead the weak Bulinskaya condition

where σℓ denotes the ℓ-dimensional Hausdorff measure on RD . Moreover, we give

and (2.4) are equivalent in the case D = d.)
    Under condition (2.4), we proved as shown in Section 4, that the level set Lu
is, almost surely, the union of a C 1 manifold and a negligible set, and thus it is
rectifiable.
   When D > d, classical proofs of KRF (see [7]) demand the strong condition (2.3).
But with the use of the Crofton formula for rectifiable sets, we are able to prove
it under the weaker condition (2.4), and again the local time argument permits to
establish it almost for free. The basis idea is the following. Let X(·) be a “nice”
random field. Then (2.4) must hold because, otherwise, the random field, “would
spend too much time in the neighborhood of u”. This last quantity is measured by
the local time: the density of the occupation measure at u.

   Some practitioners consider that the result above is sufficient for applications.
In fact, this is rarely the case: Very often, the considered level is u = 0! (as in the
seminal work of Kac [22]), and a statement, valid for almost every u, is not valid

a lot of work, especially for non-Gaussian models.

                     is Gaussian, the definition of the conditional expectation:

conditional expectation for every u. Without this work, the Kac-Rice formula for
all levels even makes no sense.
