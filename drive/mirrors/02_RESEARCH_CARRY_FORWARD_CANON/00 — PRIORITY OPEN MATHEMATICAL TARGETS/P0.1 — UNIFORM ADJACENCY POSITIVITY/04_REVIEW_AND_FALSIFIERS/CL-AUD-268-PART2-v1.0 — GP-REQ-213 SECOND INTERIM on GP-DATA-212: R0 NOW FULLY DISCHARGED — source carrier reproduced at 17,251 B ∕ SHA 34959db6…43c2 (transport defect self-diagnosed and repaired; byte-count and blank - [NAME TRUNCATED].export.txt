1.1 HOW THE TRANSPORT DEFECT WAS CAUGHT, AND WHY IT MATTERS.


The first assembled reconstruction produced 17,308 bytes with SHA-256 d1714c27… — a mismatch. It would have been easy, and wrong, to report that as an R0 version failure. Two independent discriminators showed the fault was on the reviewer's side:


  (a) BYTE COUNT. 17,308 against a declared 17,251. A genuine version difference would not be expected to land 57 bytes away while every other structural property held.
  (b) BLANK-RUN PARITY. The extraction rule requires every blank-line run to have even length. One run — exactly one, of length 3 — violated it, at payload line 234, between "return largest, largest_tail" and "def exact_spectral_witnesses(...)". That is precisely the boundary at which this reviewer had split the base64 stream for transport.


Diagnosis: one CRLF was dropped at a self-created chunk junction. Reinserting exactly one CRLF at that junction yields 17,251 bytes, all 44 runs even, and the declared SHA-256 exactly. A wrong repair could not produce a 64-hex-digit collision, so the hash is the arbiter and it settles the matter.


This is recorded in detail because the general lesson is reusable: when a carrier's extraction rule carries an internal invariant (here, blank-run parity) alongside a declared byte count, a reviewer can distinguish transport corruption from a genuine version failure instead of guessing. PART1's decision to decline rather than report a suspected mismatch was the right call, and the discriminators have now converted that abstention into a clean pass.
