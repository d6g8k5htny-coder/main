### One-to-one matching


The reusable nearest-neighbor rule allows one coarse bar to be assigned to several reference bars. Within a field, the duplicate-assignment fraction in the two shortest bands was:


- n=128: 17.5% and 19.3%
- n=256: 13.4% and 18.3%
- n=512: 9.6% and 8.5%


The maximum within-field reuse was three.


When I imposed one-to-one matching, short-bar match rates fell materially.


For lifetimes below 10^-4:


- n=128: 42.31%
- n=256: 57.69%
- n=512: 71.79%


For lifetimes in [10^-4,10^-3):


- n=128: 53.97%
- n=256: 60.32%
- n=512: 77.78%


The decrease exceeds the frozen 10-percentage-point threshold, so R3 triggers. The published “89.6% short-bar match rate” is materially inflated as evidence for persistence of individual bars by reusable nearest-neighbor matching.


The one-to-one matched bars still had very large lifetime errors. At n=512 the median relative errors were approximately 48.9 for ell<10^-4 and 3.93 for 10^-4<=ell<10^-3. The value-instability conclusion survives.


A matching cost that also weakly used log lifetime lowered the identity rate further in the shortest bands and selected bars with smaller errors, as expected; because lifetime is the quantity under test, I treat that variant only as sensitivity evidence, not as the primary match.


## Where I differ from CL-DER-018


The statement “their lifetimes are not measurable at these resolutions” is supported by the independent re-execution.


The stronger statement “the short bars are real” needs narrowing. The experiment shows a substantial population of birth neighborhoods that recur under refinement, but the original 89.6% figure does not establish one-to-one persistence of individual bars. Under a one-to-one birth-location assignment, the finest short-band rates are about 72% and 78%, not 94%.


This remains evidence that the entire population is not pure independent pixel noise. It is not yet a reliable identification of each continuum critical-point pair.
