
Three bridges from persistence thermodynamics to

geometry, entropy, and field theory

The canonical partition function Z(β) = Ʃᵢ exp(β·ℓᵢ) over persistence lifetimes {ℓᵢ} from
topological data analysis generates an unexpectedly rich mathematical structure. The Gibbs

measure πᵢ(β) = exp(β·ℓᵢ)/Z(β) is a one-parameter exponential family, and three precise
mathematical results connect it to information geometry, Rényi entropy, and Wilsonian

renormalization. What follows is the fullest available mathematical treatment of each result—

distinguishing what is proven from what is conjectured, and identifying specific gaps where

original contributions are possible.

1. The Fisher metric and exponential family structure

Chentsov’s uniqueness theorem

The Fisher information metric for the persistence Gibbs measure is gββ = ψ″(β) = Varβ[ℓ],
where ψ(β) = log Z(β) is the cumulant generating function. The foundational result justifying

this metric’s privileged status is:

Theorem (Chentsov, 1972). On the probability simplex Δₙ, the Fisher–Rao metric is the
unique Riemannian metric (up to a positive multiplicative constant) that is invariant under
sufficient statistics—equivalently, under congruent Markov embeddings.

The proof proceeds in three steps. First, define Markov embeddings: given a partition

{B₁,…,Bₘ} of {1,…,n} with conditional probability table Q, the stochastic map T: Δₙ₋₁ → Δₘ₋₁

defined by (Tp)ⱼ = Ʃᵢ Qⱼ|ᵢ pᵢ is a Markov morphism. Agustinus Kristiadi  Second, Campbell’s
lemma (1986) shows that any Riemannian metric h on ℝⁿ invariant under all such maps must
have the form hᵢⱼ(p) = c·δᵢⱼ/pᵢ + d for constants c > 0 and d. Agustinus Kristiadi  Third,
restricting to the simplex Δₙ₋₁ where tangent vectors v satisfy Ʃᵢ vᵢ = 0, the d-term vanishes,
yielding h(v,w) = c·Ʃᵢ vᵢwᵢ/pᵢ—precisely c times the Fisher metric. kristia

The theorem has been extended significantly. Ay, Jost, Lê, and Schwachhöfer (2015) proved

that both the Fisher metric and the Amari–Chentsov cubic tensor Tᵢⱼₖ = Eβ[(∂ᵢ log p)(∂ⱼ
log p)(∂ₖ log p)] are uniquely characterized (up to constants) by invariance under sufficient

statistics, for arbitrary parametric families on infinite sample spaces. ResearchGate  Dowty

(2018) gave a streamlined proof restricted to exponential families. kristia  A stronger

characterization also holds: the Fisher–Rao metric is the unique metric that is monotone



(contractive) under all stochastic maps, connecting to data-processing inequalities. PNAS

Amari’s dually flat structure

For the persistence exponential family with natural parameter θ = β and sufficient statistic

t(x) = ℓₓ, the full dually flat structure is:

Dual coordinates. The natural parameter θ = β and the expectation parameter η = Eβ[ℓ] =
ψ′(β). Since ψ″(β) > 0 (the variance is strictly positive for non-degenerate data), ψ is strictly

convex and the map β ↦ η is a diffeomorphism.

Dual potentials. The cumulant generating function ψ(β) = log Z(β) and its Legendre dual ψ*

(η) = supβ{βη − ψ(β)} = β(η)·η − ψ(β(η)). The dual potential equals the negative Shannon

entropy of the Gibbs distribution: ψ*(η) = Ʃᵢ πᵢ(β) log πᵢ(β). The Legendre relations are ψ′(β)
= η and (ψ*)′(η) = β, with ψ(β) + ψ*(η) = βη.

Dual connections. The e-connection (exponential, α = +1) ∇⁽ᵉ⁾ and m-connection (mixture,
α = −1) ∇⁽ᵐ⁾ Springer  are defined by their Christoffel symbols:

Γ⁽ᵉ⁾ᵢⱼ,ₖ = E[∂ᵢ∂ⱼ log p · ∂ₖ log p]

Γ⁽ᵐ⁾ᵢⱼ,ₖ = Γ⁽ᵉ⁾ᵢⱼ,ₖ + gᵢⱼ,ₖ (shifted by the derivative of the metric)

Theorem (Amari, 1985). Every exponential family carries a dually flat structure (M, g, ∇⁽ᵉ⁾,
∇⁽ᵐ⁾) where: (i) ∇⁽ᵉ⁾ is flat with affine coordinates θ (natural parameters); (ii) ∇⁽ᵐ⁾ is flat with
affine coordinates η (expectation parameters); (iii) gᵢⱼ = ∂²ψ/∂θⁱ∂θʲ; (iv) gⁱʲ = ∂²ψ/∂ηᵢ∂ηⱼ; (v)
the Levi-Civita connection ∇⁽⁰⁾ = ½(∇⁽ᵉ⁾ + ∇⁽ᵐ⁾).*

For the one-dimensional persistence model, both connections are trivially flat (a single

coordinate always admits flat geometry), but the dual structure still carries content through

the choice of preferred affine parameterization and the potentials ψ, ψ*.

Bregman divergence equals KL divergence

The Bregman divergence generated by the convex function ψ is:

Dψ(β₁ ‖ β₂) = ψ(β₁) − ψ(β₂) − ψ′(β₂)(β₁ − β₂) University of Florida

Theorem. For distributions π(β₁) and π(β₂) in the same exponential family, KL(π(β₁) ‖ π(β₂))
= Dψ(β₂ ‖ β₁) (note the argument swap).

The proof is direct. Write KL(π(β₁) ‖ π(β₂)) = Ʃᵢ πᵢ(β₁) log[πᵢ(β₁)/πᵢ(β₂)] = Ʃᵢ πᵢ(β₁)[(β₁ − β₂)ℓᵢ −
ψ(β₁) + ψ(β₂)] = (β₁ − β₂)ψ′(β₁) − ψ(β₁) + ψ(β₂) = ψ(β₂) − ψ(β₁) − ψ′(β₁)(β₂ − β₁) = Dψ(β₂ ‖ β₁).

The critical identity used is Eβ₁[ℓ] = ψ′(β₁). Polytechnique  In the dual η-coordinates, the
swap disappears: KL(π(β₁) ‖ π(β₂)) = Dψ*(η₁ ‖ η₂). inFERENCe  The infinitesimal limit gives



KL(π(β) ‖ π(β + dβ)) ≈ ½ψ″(β)(dβ)² = ½gββ(dβ)², recovering the Fisher metric as the

Hessian of KL divergence. This is the canonical divergence of any dually flat manifold (Amari

exponential families and regular Bregman divergences was proven by Banerjee et al. (2005).

Ruppeiner geometry requires at least two dimensions

For any one-dimensional Riemannian manifold, the Riemann curvature tensor vanishes

identically. The tensor R^a_{bcd} has n²(n² − 1)/12 independent components; for n = 1 this

gives zero. The parameter space (ℝ, gββdβ²) is always locally isometric to flat ℝ via the
substitution σ = ∫√ψ″(β) dβ.

The minimum extension for nontrivial curvature is the two-parameter family using birth

and death coordinates separately:

Z(βb, βd) = Ʃᵢ exp(βb · bᵢ + βd · dᵢ)

with natural parameters θ = (βb, βd) and sufficient statistics (bᵢ, dᵢ). The 2×2 Fisher
information matrix is the Hessian of ψ(βb, βd) = log Z(βb, βd):

βb βd

βb g₁₁ = Varβ[b] g₁₂ = Covβ[b, d]

βd g₂₁ = Covβ[b, d] g₂₂ = Varβ[d]

The scalar curvature R involves third and fourth cumulants of (b, d) through the Christoffel

symbols Γᵏᵢⱼ = ½g^{kl}ψᵢⱼₗ (containing third cumulants ψᵢⱼₖ) and their derivatives. Following
Ruppeiner’s interpretation (Rev. Mod. Phys. 67, 605, 1995): R = 0 indicates non-interacting

(ideal gas) behavior; Wikipedia  R > 0 signals effective repulsion; R < 0 signals effective

attraction; and |R| → ∞ signals a phase transition where correlation lengths diverge. arXiv

Springer  For the persistence model, R diverges when det(g) = Var[b]·Var[d] − Cov[b,d]² →

0, meaning birth and death times become perfectly correlated (|ρb,d| → 1)—a structural

phase transition in the topological data.

Thermodynamic length and the Crooks dissipation bound

The thermodynamic length along a path β(t) from β₁ to β₂ is:

L = ∫β₁^β₂ √gββ(β) dβ = ∫β₁^β₂ √Varβ[ℓ] dβ

This quantity counts the number of statistically distinguishable equilibrium states along the

path, threeplusone  connecting to Wootters’ (1981) statistical distance. Semantic Scholar



Each infinitesimal step dβ contributes √gββ dβ = one standard deviation of fluctuation in ℓ.

Crooks (Phys. Rev. Lett. 99, 100602, 2007) established the precise connection to finite-time

thermodynamics. Threeplusone  He defined the thermodynamic divergence J = τ ∫₀^τ (dθⁱ/

dt) gᵢⱼ (dθʲ/dt) dt—the “action” of the curve scaled by duration τ. The Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality yields J ≥ L², with equality only for constant-speed (geodesic) protocols.

threeplusone  Since total dissipation scales as J/N for N discrete steps (or J/(2τ) in the

continuous quasistatic limit), this gives the Salamon–Berry–Crooks bound:

⟨Wdiss⟩ ≥ L²/(2τ)

For the persistence model, this means the minimum entropy production when continuously

varying β from β₁ to β₂ over duration τ is bounded below by L². Optimal annealing schedules

in β-space follow geodesics of the Fisher metric, moving slowly where Varβ[ℓ] is large (near
phase transitions) and quickly where it is small. threeplusone

The α-connection ∇⁽α⁾ for α ∈ ℝ interpolates continuously between the e- and m-

Γ⁽α⁾ᵢⱼ,ₖ = Γ⁽⁰⁾ᵢⱼ,ₖ − (α/2) Tᵢⱼₖ

where Tᵢⱼₖ = E[∂ᵢ log p · ∂ⱼ log p · ∂ₖ log p] is the Amari–Chentsov tensor (the third cumulant
tensor). At α = 0 one recovers the Levi-Civita connection; at α = ±1 the e/m-connections.

Franknielsen  arXiv  For general α, the connections ∇⁽α⁾ and ∇⁽⁻α⁾ are dual with respect to g.

The associated α-divergences are D⁽α⁾(p ‖ q) = (4/(1−α²))[1 − Ʃᵢ pᵢ^{(1+α)/2} qᵢ^{(1−α)/2}],
recovering KL divergence as α → ±1 and the squared Hellinger distance at α = 0.

For the one-parameter persistence model, the Amari–Chentsov tensor has a single

component T₁₁₁ = κ₃(β), the third cumulant (skewness) of ℓ under the Gibbs measure. No
natural choice of α specific to persistence data has been identified in the literature.

This remains an open problem—a natural α might emerge from the geometry of persistence

diagrams, from optimal transport considerations, or from the specific moment structure of

lifetime distributions. This is a gap for original contribution.

2. Baez’s Rényi–free energy bridge

The exact theorem

John Baez proved the following identity connecting Rényi entropy to free energy

(arXiv:1102.2098; arXiv  published as Entropy 24(5):706, 2022):

Theorem (Baez, 2011/2022). For a system with Gibbs state ρ_{T₀} at temperature T₀ and
free energy F(T) = −kT ln Z(T), the Rényi entropy of order q = T₀/T equals the negative
difference quotient of free energy: ucr

S_{T₀/T}(T₀) = −[F(T) − F(T₀)]/(T − T₀)

Equivalently, the Rényi entropy is the q⁻¹-derivative (Jackson q-derivative) of negative free
energy: University of California, Riverside

Sq = −(dF/dT)_{q⁻¹}

where (df/dx)_q = [f(qx) − f(x)]/(qx − x). In the limit q → 1 this recovers the standard
thermodynamic identity S = −dF/dT. PubMed Central

Derivation in the persistence convention

In the user’s positive-β convention with Z(β) = Ʃᵢ exp(β·ℓᵢ), the Gibbs probabilities are πᵢ(β)
= exp(β·ℓᵢ)/Z(β). The Rényi entropy of order q is:

Hq(π(β)) = (1/(1−q)) log(Ʃᵢ πᵢ(β)^q)

Since πᵢ(β)^q = exp(qβ·ℓᵢ)/Z(β)^q, summing over i gives Ʃᵢ πᵢ(β)^q = Z(qβ)/Z(β)^q.
Therefore:

Hq(π(β)) = (1/(1−q)) ln[Z(qβ)/Z(β)^q]

Defining the “persistence free energy” F(β) = −(1/β) ln Z(β), so that ln Z(β) = −βF(β), this

becomes:

Hq = qβ/(q−1) · [F(qβ) − F(β)]

The derivation is elementary but the interpretation is deep. Baez emphasizes that Rényi

entropy is a secant-line approximation to the slope of −F(T), while Shannon entropy is the

tangent-line (derivative). University of California, Riverside  As Polettini noted: start with a

system in thermal equilibrium at temperature T = 1/β, quench it by multiplying β by q. The

maximum extractable work divided by the temperature change equals the Rényi entropy of

order q in the original state.



What was new versus known. The special case with F(T₀) = 0 appeared in Beck & Schlögl

(1995). Baez independently rediscovered the connection and, after Downes generalized to

F(T₀) ≠ 0, wrote it in the elegant q-derivative form. University of California, Riverside  The q-

derivative formulation—connecting to Jackson calculus and quantum groups—appears to be

Baez’s original contribution. PubMed Central  The paper does not use categorical or operadic

machinery; it is a direct thermodynamic calculation.

What varying q probes in persistence data

The Rényi entropy Hq is monotonically non-increasing in q: Wikipedia  H₀ ≥ H₁ ≥ H₂ ≥ … ≥

H∞. Each order probes a different aspect of the Gibbs-weighted lifetime distribution:

q → 0 (Hartley entropy): H₀ = ln n, where n = |{ℓᵢ}|. Counts all persistence features
equally. Measures topological richness independent of thermal weighting.

q = 1 (Shannon entropy): H₁ = −Ʃᵢ πᵢ ln πᵢ. The standard thermodynamic entropy,
balancing common and rare features. Measures average information content.

q = 2 (collision entropy): H₂ = −ln(Ʃᵢ πᵢ²). Related to the probability that two randomly
sampled features have the same Gibbs weight. Measures concentration.

q → ∞ (min-entropy): H∞ = −ln(maxᵢ πᵢ). Dominated entirely by the single most probable
feature—the longest-lived topological feature at temperature 1/β.

Via Baez’s formula, varying q is equivalent to probing free energy at different effective

inverse temperatures qβ. Small q corresponds to high effective temperature (all features

equally occupied—sensitive to richness); large q corresponds to low effective temperature

(system frozen into the dominant feature—sensitive to the core topological structure).

Hill numbers and the Pielou connection

The Hill number of order q, introduced by Hill (1973) and given its “true diversity”

interpretation by Jost ScienceDirect  (Oikos 113:363, 2006), is the exponential of Rényi
entropy:

ᵠD = (Ʃᵢ pᵢ^q)^{1/(1−q)} = exp(Hq)

Special cases: ⁰D = n (richness), ¹D = exp(H₁) (exponential Shannon entropy), ResearchGate

²D = 1/(Ʃᵢ pᵢ²) (inverse Simpson). loujost  Jost proved that all standard diversity indices that
are monotonic functions of Ʃ pᵢ^q yield the same Hill number when converted to effective
number of species, ScienceDirect  and that Hill numbers satisfy the doubling property:

splitting each species into two equally abundant groups exactly doubles ᵠD. loujost

In terms of the persistence partition function, the Hill number takes a striking form as a ratio

of partition functions:



ᵠD(β) = [Z(qβ)/Z(β)^q]^{1/(1−q)}

The Hill evenness E_{q,0} = ᵠD/⁰D = exp(Hq)/n measures how evenly the Gibbs weight is
distributed. R Project  The relationship to Pielou’s evenness J = H₁/ln n R Project  is:

E₁,₀ = exp(H₁)/n = exp(J·ln n)/n = n^{J−1}

When J = 1 (maximal evenness), E₁,₀ = 1. When J → 0, E₁,₀ → 1/n. Jost (2010) showed that

Pielou’s J is the relative logarithmic evenness RLE₀,₁ = H₁/ln n—geometrically, the slope of

the chord of the Rényi spectrum from q = 0 to q = 1. MDPI

The unexplored (β, q) diagnostic surface

The two-parameter family Hq(β) defines a surface over the (β, q) half-plane, where β

controls energy-scale weighting (which persistence features are thermally activated) and

q controls moment-order sensitivity (emphasis on common versus rare features). No

published work studies this surface in the TDA context. The standard “persistent entropy”

literature (Atienza et al. 2020) uses Shannon entropy ACM Digital Library  ScienceDirect  (q =

1) with uniform normalization (effectively β → 0), capturing neither the thermal weighting

nor the Rényi generalization. The (β, q) surface is an open area for original contribution,

with the Hill number ᵠD(β) = [Z(qβ)/Z(β)^q]^{1/(1−q)} interpretable as the “effective number
of thermally active features” at temperature 1/β and moment order q.

Tsallis versus Rényi: deformed thermodynamics

The Tsallis entropy Sq^Tsallis = (1 − Ʃᵢ pᵢ^q)/(q − 1) is a monotonic transform of Rényi
entropy: Sq^Tsallis = (1 − exp((1−q)Hq))/(q−1). They carry identical information and are

maximized by the same distribution. The key difference is additivity: Rényi entropy is

additive for independent systems (Hq(A⊗B) = Hq(A) + Hq(B)), while Tsallis entropy is

pseudo-additive (Sq(A⊗B) = Sq(A) + Sq(B) + (1−q)Sq(A)Sq(B)).

The Tsallis partition function Zq(β) = Ʃᵢ expq(β·ℓᵢ) = Ʃᵢ [1 + (1−q)β·ℓᵢ]₊^{1/(1−q)} uses the q-
exponential, Wikipedia  yielding power-law (generalized Pareto) distributions rather than

exponential Boltzmann distributions. NCBI  The escort distribution Pᵢ^(q) = pᵢ^q/Ʃⱼ pⱼ^q
reweights by amplifying (q > 1) or suppressing (q < 1) the tails. MDPI  As Baez emphasized,

Rényi entropy already has a natural role in standard statistical mechanics as a ratio of free

energies at different temperatures—there is no need for the deformed formalism. As

Leinster noted, the fundamental quantity is arguably the Hill number exp(Hq), not the

entropy itself. Azimuth

3. Sonoda–Suzuki: gradient flow is Wilsonian RG



The exact theorem

Sonoda and Suzuki proved the following correspondence between gradient flow and the

exact renormalization group (PTEP 2019, 033B05; arXiv:1901.05169):

Theorem (Sonoda–Suzuki, 2019). Consider a real scalar field φ(x) in D-dimensional
Euclidean space, with gradient flow (diffusion) ∂φt/∂t = ∇²φt and φ₀ = φ. In the ERG
formalism with Gaussian cutoff function K(p²/Λ²) = exp(−p²/Λ²), the following identity holds
exactly:

⟨φt(x₁) φt(x₂) ⋯ φt(xₙ)⟩{S_bare} = ⟨φ(x₁) φ(x₂) ⋯ φ(xₙ)⟩{S_Λ}

where the left side computes correlation functions of the diffused fields using the bare
action, and the right side computes correlation functions of the bare fields using the
Wilsonian effective action S_Λ at cutoff Λ, with the identification:

Λ² = 1/(2t), equivalently Λ = 1/σ where σ = √(2t) is the smoothing scale.

The proof works in momentum space. The diffused field is φ̃t(p) = e^{−tp²}φ̃(p), which is

Gaussian smoothing—convolution with a heat kernel of width σ = √(2t). With the specific

Gaussian cutoff K(p²/Λ²) = exp(−p²/Λ²), the ERG blocking step produces exactly the same

momentum-space damping factor, establishing the identity at the level of generating

functionals. Oxford Academic

Precise conditions. The result requires: (i) a generic real scalar field theory arXiv  (not

necessarily renormalizable); (ii) the specific Gaussian ERG cutoff K = exp(−p²/Λ²)—other

cutoff choices modify the flow away from simple diffusion; (iii) a linear gradient flow (∂φt/∂t

= ∇²φt contains no interaction terms). Abe and Fukuma (2018, arXiv:1805.12094)

generalized this: for arbitrary cutoff functions, the diffused field obeys a generalized

diffusion equation determined by the cutoff. ResearchGate

The result is exact and non-perturbative within the ERG formalism—not an approximation.

It holds at the full quantum level for the scalar theory in the continuum.

The Polchinski equation as a functional heat equation

The connection runs deeper than the specific Sonoda–Suzuki theorem. Polchinski’s (1984)

ERG equation for the interaction part of the Wilsonian action Sunysb  reads:

Λ ∂Sᵢₙₜ/∂Λ = ∫p [Λ ∂CΛ(p)/∂Λ] × {(δSᵢₙₜ/δφp)(δSᵢₙₜ/δφ₋p) − δ²Sᵢₙₜ/(δφp δφ₋p)}

where CΛ(p) is the cutoff propagator. As Rosten emphasized (Phys. Rept. 511:177, 2012),

rewriting in terms of the Boltzmann weight ψ = exp(−Sᵢₙₜ), this becomes:

Λ ∂ψ/∂Λ = ∫p [Λ ∂CΛ/∂Λ] · δ²ψ/(δφp δφ₋p)



This is a functional diffusion equation—a heat equation in the infinite-dimensional space

of field configurations, with “time” variable ln Λ and a field-dependent diffusion coefficient.

The nonlinear (δS/δφ)² term in Polchinski’s equation—which encodes interactions—becomes

the linear second-derivative term after the exponential change of variables. Rosten showed
that this heat equation structure can be exploited to prove the quantized spectrum of

anomalous dimensions at critical fixed points. arXiv

In the free-field limit (Sᵢₙₜ = 0), the ERG equation reduces exactly to the ordinary heat
equation for the field, which is the core of the Sonoda–Suzuki correspondence. The

gradient flow is the free-field (kinetic/Gaussian) part of the Wilsonian RG. Oxford Academic
Under the local potential approximation (LPA)—where the Wilsonian action is restricted to

S_Λ[φ] = ∫d^D x [½(∂µφ)² + VΛ(φ)]—the functional PDE collapses to a standard PDE for the

potential V(φ, Λ), which at leading order is again a heat equation in the field variable φ with

“time” related to ln Λ. Rabambi (2024, arXiv:2406.12523) recently made this LPA reduction

fully explicit for both bosonic and fermionic fields. arXiv

Persistence diagrams under gradient flow

For a scalar field f smoothed at scale σ via convolution with a Gaussian kernel, the

bottleneck stability theorem (Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer, 2007) guarantees that

persistence diagrams change continuously: dB(Dgm(f), Dgm(fσ)) ≤ ‖f − fσ‖∞, where dB is

the bottleneck distance. Qualitatively:

Features with lifetime ℓ ≪ σ are destroyed as smoothing removes fluctuations below the
smoothing scale, pushing points in the persistence diagram toward the diagonal. Features

with ℓ ≫ σ are preserved, with birth/death values shifted by at most ‖f − fσ‖∞. No rigorous
flow equation exists for individual persistence lifetimes ℓᵢ(σ). The persistence diagram
changes discontinuously at critical σ values where birth-death events occur. The vineyard

description (Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Morozov, 2006) tracks how persistence pairs

evolve under continuous parameter changes, but does not yield a smooth ODE for each ℓᵢ.

For Gaussian random fields, the expected number of critical points (hence persistence

features) decreases as σ increases, following predictions from the Kac–Rice formula, with

characteristic lifetime scaling with σ.

Evidence from lattice gauge theory

Sale, Lucini, and Giansiracusa (Phys. Rev. D 107, 034501, 2023; arXiv:2207.13392) applied

persistent homology to SU(2) lattice gauge theory, constructing gauge-invariant filtrations

from Polyakov loop angle differences. They demonstrated that persistence-based

observables are directly sensitive to center vortices—the topological objects believed to

drive confinement—and extracted critical coupling βc and critical exponent ν via finite-size

scaling of persistence indicators.



Spitz, Urban, and Pawlowski (Phys. Rev. D 107, 034506, 2023; arXiv:2208.03955)

conducted a comprehensive persistent homology analysis of SU(2) using multiple

observables (Polyakov loops, topological densities, field strengths). Under Wilson flow

cooling, they found that most persistence features are stable—the main structural

signatures survive smoothing, with cooling enhancing signals of classical topological

objects (instanton-dyons) while removing UV noise. Their key observation: “main results are

barely affected by the cooling procedure,” meaning TDA observables already capture IR-

relevant physics from the full quantum ensemble without needing gradient flow. In follow-up

work on SU(3) (arXiv:2412.09112), they discovered signals of electromagnetic duality in

persistent homology of electric and magnetic field energy densities.

The (σ, β) bifiltration and the missing beta function

The proposed bifiltration with σ (Wilsonian smoothing scale, Λ = 1/σ) and β (thermal

weighting in Z(β)) defines a two-parameter family of persistence-derived observables. A

crossover line β*(σ)—where the partition function transitions from being dominated by many

short-lived features to few long-lived features—has not been defined or studied. The

existing multiparameter persistence literature (degree-Rips, multicover, core bifiltrations)

does not combine a smoothing parameter with Boltzmann-type thermal weighting.

Similarly, no flow equation of the form dJ₀/dσ = βJ(J₀, σ) for Pielou evenness under

smoothing exists. The fundamental obstacle is that persistence diagrams change

discontinuously at critical smoothing scales, making smooth ODEs for scalar summaries

problematic except perhaps in an expected-value or distributional sense. A stochastic flow

equation (Fokker–Planck type) for persistence statistics, in the spirit of Carosso’s

stochastic RG (JHEP 01, 2020, 172), might be more natural than a deterministic beta

function. This entire direction—RG flow equations for persistence-based observables—

represents a significant opportunity for original contribution.

Open gaps for original contributions. Five specific opportunities stand out. First, the (β,

q) diagnostic surface for persistence diagrams—combining Gibbs weighting with Rényi

order to define Hill numbers ᵠD(β) as “effective number of thermally active topological
features”—is entirely unstudied. Second, the (σ, β) bifiltration combining Wilsonian

smoothing with thermal weighting, including the crossover line β*(σ), has no precedent.

Third, a natural α-connection for persistence data has not been identified. Fourth, flow

equations for persistence observables under gradient flow—whether deterministic beta

functions or stochastic Fokker–Planck equations for Pielou evenness, Hill numbers, or other

summaries—do not exist. Fifth, the Bregman-divergence-based clustering of persistence

diagrams via the exponential family structure of Z(β), connecting to Banerjee et al.’s (2005)

framework, is unexplored.

The mathematical infrastructure is complete and rigorous. What remains is the synthesis:

applying these three proven bridges—information geometry, Rényi–free energy duality, and

gradient flow RG—simultaneously to the persistence partition function, creating a unified (σ,

β, q) framework for multiscale topological analysis of scalar random fields.
