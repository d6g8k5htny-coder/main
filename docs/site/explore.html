01 / THE SHAPE NEAR A POINT

When is a hill really a peak?

At a stationary point, the slope is zero. What matters next is how the surface bends. If it bends downward in every direction, the point is a local peak. A saddle bends upward in one direction and downward in another.

Why this idea appears in the research

A Hessian is a matrix that records curvature. The coefficient calculation sorts matrices by their curvature signs, then averages a weighted contribution over the peak-shaped ones. The picture lets you inspect that selection rule.

See the mathematics, source and limits

The source writes the two-by-two transverse Hessian as A = [[s + x, y], [y, s − x]], with R = √(x² + y²). Its eigenvalues are s − R and s + R. Negative definiteness is exactly s < −R. At a zero eigenvalue, the second-order test is inconclusive; higher-order terms can matter.

02 / TWO DIFFERENT KINDS OF SMALL

Closer together. Much closer in height.

Call the distance between two pinned critical points r. In the research model, their height gap is proportional to r³. Halving the distance divides that gap by eight. Meanwhile, different regions of space can shrink—or stay fixed.

Try this: compare r = 0.50 with r = 0.25. Then switch the highlighted region.

A small distinction with a big effect

The fixed-annulus argument concerns a shrinking ring and allows all heights. The fixed-remote argument keeps a spatial exclusion fixed and counts within the shrinking height window. Their conditions answer different questions.

See the mathematics, sources and limits

The diagram fixes A = 2, B = 4, ρ = 3, L = 24, b = 1 and k = 1 to illustrate the definitions. Pins are M = (−r/2, 0) and S = (r/2, 0). The annulus is Ar ≤ |x| ≤ Br; the remote region satisfies dist(x, 0) ≥ ρ; the remote height window is b − kr³ < f(x) < b.

03 / UNDERSTANDING AN OBSTRUCTION

Three objects. Two places. One impossible fit.

Suppose you have two groups, and each group can hold at most one object. Can all three objects be assigned? What if you remove any one of them?

Try this: remove any one object. Put it back, then remove a different one.

Choose the objects to include

The rule stays the same: one object per group.

Use Tab to reach an object and Space to select it.

CAPACITY ONE · TWO GROUPS

One object has no place

Three included objects need three places, but only two are available. Removing any one object makes a valid assignment possible.

The whole set can fail while every smaller set works

That makes the triple a minimal obstruction: it fails the rule, but removing anything repairs it. This tiny example is a starting point for the project’s finite-family work on covers and their cost.

See the mathematics, source and limits

This reuses the museum’s finite P15 model: one original block, capacity a = 1, demand d = 2, n = 3 coordinates, K = 2 palette classes and no macro edges. A valid group is a member of the capacity family; a valid assignment partitions the selected objects into at most two such groups.

The full triple is not two-decomposable, and every proper subset is. This exact finite example illustrates one realized family. It does not establish the full price theorem, extend to arbitrary families, or close a prize problem.
