A READING PATH

What can the shape
of a landscape tell us?

The program connects probability, geometry, and topology. Start with a question below. Each path leads from an intuitive example to the precise argument and its recorded review.

This is a selection of source-bound reading paths, not a live list of all completed results. The status record and current proof index describe later developments.

01 / PERSISTENCE

How long does a feature last?

As a height threshold moves, a landscape’s connected regions appear and merge. Persistence records how long each feature survives in threshold space. A lifetime theorem needs more than a picture: it must identify the relevant event and control the probabilistic terms that describe it.

The lifetime-remainder argument studies a particular remainder term. Its recorded review accepts an existential O(1) remainder under the stated imported assumptions. It does not supply numerical constants, a usable radius, or a second coefficient.

02 / LOCAL GEOMETRY

What does a peak look like up close?

A smooth surface bends near a critical point. The eigenvalues of its Hessian describe that bending. Two negative eigenvalues identify a strict local maximum in a nondegenerate two-dimensional example.

The SIDE24 calculation integrates a particular geometric quantity and supplies exact coefficient enclosures in dimensions two and three. Its coefficient review has a precise scope; interpreting the coefficient as part of a lifetime law retains that law’s separate assumptions.

03 / NEARBY CRITICAL POINTS

Close together is a different problem.

When two specified critical points approach, the scale matters. A region kept a fixed distance away and a region shrinking with the pair require different estimates. The interactive example shows that distinction and the cubic height-gap scale.

The linked fixed-annulus argument treats its stated planar region, with fixed annulus ratios and compact positive-gap marks. That source’s review does not cover every spatial region or close the entire RN program. Later regional results belong to their own sources.

04 / FINITE STRUCTURES

A small example can test a big idea.

Three objects and two capacity-one groups are enough to show that every smaller selection can fit even when the whole selection cannot. The example makes the role of the realized family and its capacity constraints tangible.

The full-price theorem concerns its stated disjoint-capacity family, with demands at least two, independent probabilities and a common palette. Its scope does not extend to arbitrary downsets or the separate demand-one counterexample.
